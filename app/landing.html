01

The cheap model is fine, until it isn't

Calibrated on the eval set, an 8B model is right about nine times in ten — and you can't tell which answer is the tenth from the outside. That gap is the entire opportunity: one model forces you to pay 100× more for "what is 2+2", or ship the wrong answer one time in ten on the questions that actually matter.

02

How a prompt moves through it

The routing decision costs nothing: difficulty is classified with sentence embeddings before any model is called. Only the cheapest tier's answer gets a real judge call — from a small model, not the expensive one — and later tiers fail rarely enough that judging them cost more than it saved.

01

Classify

Embedding match against a labelled set decides easy, medium or hard. ~7ms, no model call.

02

Route

Each difficulty starts at the cheapest tier whose calibrated score clears 0.80 on it. The map is derived, not hardcoded.

03

Score

A learned scorer reads the cheap model's own token confidence — free, it's metadata on the answer already generated — and outputs P(correct).

04

Verify

Above the confidence threshold the answer ships as-is. Everything else pays $0.00004 for a verdict from a small judge model.

05

Escalate

Only when the check fails. 26 of 116 queries escalated; the other 90 were answered where they started.

Why a scorer and a judge

FrugalGPT's idea is to replace the LLM judge with a small learned model. We built that, then measured it against the judge on the same answers: the judge catches 94% of wrong cheap answers, the scorer about half. So the scorer never replaces the judge — it decides when the judge is needed. The accept threshold is set at the highest band where zero wrong answers slipped through in held-out testing (0 of 15 at P ≥ 0.95); below it, the judge rules.

What that saves depends entirely on what a verdict costs relative to the answer it protects. Here the judge is 2% of a mid-tier answer, so the gate trims a few percent of verifications. Point the judge at the tier above instead — where a verdict costs about what the next answer would — and the same code is the difference between a cascade that pays for itself and one that ties its own mid tier. We measured both.

03

What it actually saved

Every number on this page comes out of the project's own eval runs and regenerates with the data — there are no illustrative figures here. Including the uncomfortable history: the first stack tried tied its own mid-tier model exactly, because the judge was the mid model and verification cost what it saved. Two changes — a cheap tier that clears the bar on more questions, and a judge 50× cheaper than a mid answer — turned that tie into 41% off with every graded answer correct. The README has the arithmetic.

04

Bring your own models

Connect a provider, list its models, and calibrate them. Calibration runs a stratified set of objectively-scoreable questions and scores each difficulty band separately — those scores are the routing map. Change the models and the map re-derives itself.

per model

Calibrate once

Measurements belong to the model, not the slot. Use the same model as cheap in one session and mid in another without re-measuring.

your choice

Keys stay yours

By default a provider key lives in your browser for the session and is never written to disk. Opt in and it's encrypted at rest instead.

per session

Pinned models

A chat freezes the three models it was created with, so its history stays coherent when you reconfigure later.

OpenAI-shaped

One endpoint

POST your messages and a tier→model map. You get a normal completion back, plus what it routed to and what it saved.

# route a request through your own models
curl -X POST https://your-host/api/v1/route \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer rtr_your_key" \
  -d '{
    "messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "What is 2+2?"}],
    "models": {"cheap": "groq/openai/gpt-oss-20b", "frontier": "openai/gpt-4o"}
  }'
